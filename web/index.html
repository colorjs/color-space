Use it

One package for JavaScript: import a single space for under 1.5 kB, or all of them to convert any space to any other. The same conversions run as WASM over whole images and as GLSL or WGSL in shaders, and export as LUTs and ICC profiles. Agents call them through its MCP server.

What is a color space?

A coordinate system for color: axes, ranges, and a rule tying the numbers to measurable light. The same orange is rgb(255 128 0), oklch(0.732 0.186 53) and XYZ [49, 36.7, 4.5] – the same color, written three ways. A palette is a handful of chosen colors; a space is a way to write down any color at all.

Color model or color space – what's the difference?

A color model is a shape for the numbers: RGB says three primaries, HSL says hue, saturation and lightness, CMYK says four inks. A color space ties a model to measurable light – which primaries, which white, which curve – so each set of numbers names exactly one color. rgb(255 0 0) is a different red in sRGB, Display P3 and Adobe RGB: one model, three spaces.

Everyday usage calls both spaces – so does CSS's color-mix(in hsl, …), and so does this catalog. A model without an anchor of its own borrows one: HSL and HSV here reshape sRGB, as in CSS.

Which space should I actually use?

It depends on the task:

  • storing and sharing images – sRGB, the web's default;
  • designing palettes and gradients – OKLCH, where equal steps look equal;
  • math on light (blending, blurring, 3D) – linear-light RGB;
  • shooting and grading video – your camera's log space, delivered in Rec. 709 or Rec. 2100;
  • measuring how different two colors look – CIELAB or DIN99o.

Grouping the catalog by purpose shelves it the same way.

What should I use instead of HSL?

OKLCH. It keeps the same three controls – hue, chroma, lightness – but its lightness is trustworthy: in HSL, yellow at L=50 emits about 13 times the light of blue at L=50, so equal L doesn't mean equal brightness. In OKLCH it does. CSS supports oklch() natively.

Why do my gradients turn muddy in the middle?

Because they're computed in gamma-encoded sRGB, where averaging two colors' numbers produces darker, duller light than actually mixing their light would. Interpolating in OKLab avoids this. Every dossier's interpolate row draws the same blend through two spaces side by side, so you can see the difference before choosing.

What does “out of gamut” mean?

A color one space can describe but another – or your screen – can't show. A vivid Display P3 green simply doesn't exist in sRGB: convert it, and the numbers fall outside the valid range. The atlas keeps this visible: the pill next to the current color names the tightest standard gamut that holds it, and out-of-range stretches ghost over a checkerboard.

Why does print look duller than the screen?

A screen emits light; paper reflects what its inks don't absorb. Ink on paper reaches fewer vivid colors – saturated RGB blues, greens and reds fall outside it – so they print duller. Prepress converts through an ICC profile made for the press and paper, such as FOGRA39 for European coated stock or GRACoL in the US. This catalog's CMYK is the plain formula CSS uses for device-cmyk() without a profile – right for the numbers, not a proof of the print.

Are conversions exact? Do round trips lose data?

Conversions are closed formulas, not lookup approximations, and round trips return within floating-point noise. The few exceptions are documented where they occur: OSA-UCS has no closed inverse, atlas systems like Munsell interpolate between published grades, and rgb output clamps to the sRGB gamut – route through XYZ or linear RGB when you need values outside it.

They are also checked: every space links the paper or spec it implements, values are tested against colorjs.io wherever the two overlap, and the camera logs against the Academy's official ACES transforms. formula-verification.md records what was checked against what – including a few places where the published papers themselves had errors.

If the numbers differ from another library's, compare ranges: this one keeps each space's conventional ranges – RGB 0–255, Lab lightness 0–100, hue 0–360 – where many flatten everything to 0–1. Every card shows its ranges; in code they're space.oklch.range.

Which of these can CSS write?

Natively rgb(), hsl(), hwb(), lab(), lch(), oklab(), oklch(), plus color() for Display P3, Adobe RGB, ProPhoto, linear sRGB and XYZ (D65 and D50). CSS's rec2020 is a pure 2.4 gamma, not the BT.2020 curve of this catalog's Rec. 2020 – the same numbers would name a different color. Each dossier's css tab writes the current color in copyable form; spaces CSS lacks ship as ICC profiles or LUTs instead.

Where's Pantone, NCS, RAL?

Those are licensed products: their colors are defined by data the owners sell, not by published formulas – so there's no open math to implement, and redistributing their tables wouldn't be legal. Close relatives whose math is public are here: Munsell (its 1943 renotation is public record) and RAL Design (defined directly in CIELAB). The rule of thumb: if the math was ever published, it belongs in the collection.
